Vibration Mounting and Controls: Engineering Guide to Vibration Isolation Systems

anitaa September 30, 2026

Mechanical equipment rarely operates without generating some level of dynamic force. Motors rotate, pumps develop hydraulic forces, fans create aerodynamic excitation, compressors produce reciprocating loads, and industrial machinery can transmit substantial vibration into its supporting structure. When those forces reach floors, foundations, structural framing, piping, ductwork, or occupied spaces, the result can range from objectionable noise to equipment fatigue, structural response, reduced service life, and interference with sensitive operations.

This is where vibration mounting and controls become an engineering problem rather than a simple hardware selection exercise. A vibration mount is the physical interface between equipment and its support, but effective vibration control requires understanding the complete source-path-receiver system. Engineers must consider equipment weight, operating speed, excitation frequency, dynamic loading, stiffness, damping, static deflection, structural conditions, connected MEP systems, environmental exposure, and, where applicable, seismic requirements.

Different applications require different approaches. Spring vibration isolators may be appropriate where substantial static deflection and low-frequency isolation are required. Rubber or elastomeric mounts can provide compact isolation and load distribution. Wire rope isolators can be useful for equipment exposed to demanding vibration, shock, marine, or industrial environments. Acoustic hangers and floor vibration isolators address other transmission paths, particularly where structure-borne noise is a concern.

For U.S. commercial, industrial, healthcare, and infrastructure projects, vibration isolation must also be coordinated with structural and seismic requirements. ASCE 7, the IBC, CBC, and applicable HCAI requirements may affect equipment anchorage, seismic restraints, support structures, and nonstructural components, even though these codes are not general vibration-isolation standards.

A properly engineered system therefore connects vibration mechanics with equipment mounting, structural support, MEP coordination, seismic restraint, fabrication, and installation. The objective is not simply to place an isolator under a machine, but to create a controlled mechanical interface that addresses the actual operating conditions and project requirements.

What Are Vibration Mounting and Controls?

Vibration mounting refers to the hardware and structural interface used to support equipment while limiting the transmission of dynamic forces into the supporting structure. Vibration controls are broader: they include the engineering methods used to reduce, redirect, absorb, or isolate unwanted mechanical vibration.

The distinction matters because a mounting component can be mechanically adequate while the overall vibration-control strategy remains ineffective. For example, an air handling unit may be installed on properly rated spring isolators, but rigidly connected ductwork, piping, electrical conduit, or structural members can create parallel transmission paths. Vibration can then bypass the intended isolation interface and enter the building through those connections.

A useful way to understand the problem is the source-path-receiver model. The source is the equipment generating dynamic excitation. The path includes the equipment supports, isolators, floor, foundation, structural framing, piping, ductwork, and other connected systems. The receiver may be an occupied room, laboratory, medical imaging area, office, manufacturing process, or sensitive instrument. Effective vibration control considers all three.

Vibration Mounting vs. Vibration Control

A vibration mount is generally a component or assembly located between equipment and its support. Its mechanical properties can include stiffness, damping, load capacity, static deflection, and directional response.

Vibration control encompasses the larger design strategy. It may include isolation mounts, inertia bases, flexible connectors, floating floors, acoustic hangers, structural support modifications, equipment alignment, balancing, and other measures.

The right approach begins with identifying the actual source of vibration. An isolation system cannot correct every mechanical problem. Severe imbalance, misalignment, worn bearings, improper operating conditions, or resonance within the equipment itself may require mechanical correction before isolation is considered.

Why Equipment Mounting Matters

Equipment mounting affects both mechanical performance and structural response. A pump with uneven load distribution may impose different forces on each mounting point. A rooftop fan may transmit vibration through a relatively flexible roof structure. A compressor may generate dynamic forces that require a mounting system with different characteristics from those used beneath a conventional HVAC unit.

For this reason, equipment mounting should be evaluated as part of the complete mechanical and structural design rather than treated as an isolated accessory.

How Vibration Isolation Reduces Mechanical Vibration

Vibration isolation works by changing the mechanical relationship between an excitation source and the structure supporting it. Instead of providing a rigid connection that directly transfers dynamic forces, an isolation system introduces controlled stiffness and damping.

The central engineering variables are natural frequency, excitation frequency, static deflection, damping, and transmissibility. Understanding their relationship is essential when evaluating vibration isolation systems.

The Source-Path-Receiver Model

Mechanical vibration can originate from rotating imbalance, reciprocating components, aerodynamic forces, hydraulic pulsation, gear meshing, impacts, or other dynamic phenomena. Once generated, those forces travel through whatever structural and mechanical paths are available.

A rigid equipment support generally provides a direct path into the structure. An appropriately designed isolation system can reduce force transmission over the frequency range where isolation is required.

Connected services must also be considered. Pipes attached directly to vibrating equipment can transmit dynamic forces into structural supports. Duct connections can create rigid bridges between isolated equipment and building systems. Electrical connections, control wiring, and other attachments can have similar effects when improperly detailed.

Natural Frequency and Resonance

Every mass-and-stiffness system has a natural frequency. For a simplified single-degree-of-freedom system, natural frequency is related to stiffness and supported mass. Increasing supported mass or reducing effective stiffness generally lowers natural frequency.

Isolation becomes effective when the excitation frequency is sufficiently separated from the isolator's natural frequency. Conversely, operation near resonance can amplify motion rather than reduce it.

This is particularly important for low-speed machinery and variable-speed equipment. An isolator selected only by static load capacity may perform poorly if its dynamic characteristics are incompatible with the equipment's operating range.

Transmissibility and Isolation Efficiency

Transmissibility describes how vibration or dynamic force is transferred through an isolation system. It is influenced by the ratio between excitation frequency and isolation-system natural frequency, as well as damping.

The practical implication is straightforward: engineers should not evaluate an isolator solely by its maximum load rating. Two mounts with similar load capacity can have substantially different dynamic performance because their stiffness and damping characteristics differ.

For equipment operating across a range of speeds, the design should consider startup, shutdown, normal operation, and variable-frequency-drive conditions. The isolation system must function across the relevant operating envelope rather than at a single nominal RPM.

Types of Vibration Mounting Systems

No single vibration mounting technology is appropriate for every application. Selection depends on equipment mass, operating frequency, required isolation, available space, environmental exposure, structural conditions, lateral stability, and project-specific requirements.

Spring Vibration Isolators

Spring isolators are widely used for HVAC and mechanical equipment where substantial static deflection and relatively low natural frequencies are desirable. Applications can include air handling units, fans, pumps, chillers, compressors, and other equipment.

Open spring isolators provide vertical flexibility, while restrained or captive configurations can incorporate features intended to control equipment movement. The engineering design must account for spring rate, supported load, static deflection, leveling, equipment geometry, and required restraint.

Spring systems are particularly useful when low-frequency isolation is required, but their performance depends on proper load distribution and installation. A spring selected for an average equipment weight may not perform correctly if actual loading is uneven.

Rubber and Elastomeric Mounts

Rubber vibration mounts use elastomeric materials to provide stiffness and damping between equipment and its support. Natural rubber, synthetic rubber, neoprene, and other elastomeric compounds can be formulated for different operating environments and mechanical characteristics.

Rubber/metal isolators combine elastomeric elements with steel components to create compact mounting assemblies. They are commonly considered where space is limited, loads are moderate, or a more compact mounting arrangement is desirable.

Material selection should account for temperature, chemicals, moisture, UV exposure, oil, corrosion, and expected service life. Elastomer performance can change with environmental conditions, making application-specific material selection important.

Wire Rope Isolators

Wire rope isolators use formed metal cable elements to provide multi-directional compliance and vibration or shock isolation. Their construction can make them useful in demanding industrial, marine, aerospace, and specialized equipment applications.

Because wire rope systems can accommodate movement in multiple directions, they may be considered where equipment experiences combined vibration and shock loads. Stainless steel construction can also be advantageous in environments where corrosion resistance is important.

Acoustic Hangers and Floor Isolation

Suspended HVAC systems can transmit vibration through hanger rods and structural attachments. Acoustic hangers introduce an isolation element into the suspension path to reduce structure-borne vibration and associated noise transmission.

Floor vibration isolators and floating floor systems address another configuration. These systems can be relevant in laboratories, equipment rooms, studios, healthcare spaces, manufacturing environments, and other areas where floor-borne vibration must be controlled.

The selection process should therefore begin with the vibration path and required performance rather than the product category alone.

How to Select the Right Vibration Mount

Selecting a vibration mount requires more than matching an equipment weight to a catalog load range. Engineers need to establish the dynamic requirements of the application and understand how the mount interacts with the equipment and structure.

Equipment Weight and Load Distribution

Start with the actual operating weight, not merely the dry shipping weight. Equipment may contain fluids, accessories, filters, refrigerant, process materials, or other components that change its operating mass.

The center of gravity and mounting-point locations are equally important. If four mounts support a machine with an offset center of gravity, the load may not be distributed equally. Each isolator should therefore be evaluated for its actual supported load.

Mounting geometry also affects stability. Tall equipment, equipment with a high center of gravity, and machines subject to overturning forces may require additional lateral restraint or a different support configuration.

Operating Speed and Excitation Frequency

RPM is one of the most important pieces of information for rotating equipment because operating speed establishes a primary excitation frequency. However, equipment can generate multiple harmonic frequencies and dynamic forces.

Variable-speed equipment requires special attention. A fan driven by a variable-frequency drive may pass through several operating frequencies during startup and normal operation. An isolation system that performs adequately at one speed may encounter different dynamic behavior elsewhere in the operating range.

Static Deflection and Natural Frequency

Static deflection is closely related to the stiffness of an isolation system. For a simplified spring-mass system, greater static deflection generally corresponds to lower vertical natural frequency.

This does not mean that maximum deflection is automatically the correct design objective. Excessive flexibility can introduce unwanted equipment movement, stability problems, or coordination difficulties. The desired performance must balance isolation with equipment stability and project constraints.

Vertical and Horizontal Stiffness

Vibration is not necessarily vertical. Equipment can experience horizontal forces, torsional motion, startup torque, seismic movement, and other dynamic effects.

Therefore, a mounting system should be evaluated in all relevant directions. Horizontal stiffness and restraint can be particularly important for equipment located on rooftops, platforms, or elevated structures.

Damping and Environmental Conditions

Damping affects resonance behavior and transient response. The required damping characteristics depend on the equipment and performance objectives.

Environmental conditions also influence material selection. A mount installed in a clean indoor mechanical room may face very different conditions from one installed outdoors, near saltwater, in a manufacturing plant, or aboard marine equipment.

For complex applications, engineering selection should consider load, frequency, stiffness, damping, environment, geometry, and structural interface together.

Vibration Control for HVAC and MEP Equipment

HVAC and MEP equipment presents a particularly important vibration-control challenge because mechanical systems are physically connected to large portions of a building. A vibration source can therefore transmit energy through floors, roofs, piping, ductwork, electrical systems, and structural supports.

Chillers, Pumps, Fans, and Air Handling Units

Centrifugal and axial fans generate dynamic forces associated with rotating assemblies and airflow. Pumps can transmit vibration through rotating components and hydraulic systems. Chillers and air handling units combine substantial equipment mass with multiple connected services.

Isolation strategies may include spring vibration isolators, rubber mounts, inertia bases, acoustic hangers, or combinations of these systems. The appropriate configuration depends on equipment size, operating frequency, support conditions, and required isolation performance.

An inertia base can add mass and stiffness to an equipment assembly, helping address equipment stability and load distribution while providing a defined mounting interface for isolators.

Piping and Ductwork Connections

A properly isolated machine can still transmit vibration through rigidly connected piping. Flexible connectors may therefore be required to preserve the intended isolation interface.

The same principle applies to ductwork. Connections should be evaluated for flexibility, movement, and structural attachment so that the duct system does not unintentionally bypass the isolation system.

Electrical and control connections should also be coordinated where movement is expected. The objective is to avoid creating unintended rigid bridges.

Rooftop and Mechanical Room Applications

Rooftop equipment introduces additional structural considerations because roof framing may be more flexible than a ground-supported foundation. Dynamic equipment forces can become noticeable in occupied spaces below.

Mechanical-room applications can also transmit vibration through structural floors and walls. The design should consider equipment support, housekeeping pads, inertia bases, floor stiffness, and the location of vibration-sensitive spaces.

For complex MEP projects, vibration isolation should be coordinated with structural engineering and mechanical design early enough to prevent conflicts during construction.

Engineering Coordination: Structure, Equipment, and Isolation Interfaces

Vibration control becomes most effective when the equipment, isolation system, support structure, and connected services are designed as an integrated assembly. The supporting structure must be capable of carrying static and dynamic loads, while the isolation system must provide the required mechanical characteristics without creating instability or coordination conflicts.

Equipment Bases and Support Structures

Equipment may be supported directly on structural floors, housekeeping pads, steel frames, roof curbs, equipment platforms, or inertia bases. Each condition changes the load path.

An inertia base, for example, can provide a rigid equipment mounting platform while isolators separate that assembly from the building structure. A fabricated steel frame can accommodate equipment with unusual mounting geometry or provide a controlled interface between multiple machines and isolation components.

Support plates, brackets, anchor bolts, and structural members must be designed for their applicable loads. The vibration isolator should not be treated independently from the structure carrying it.

Structural Response and Dynamic Loads

The structural system itself can respond dynamically. A flexible floor may amplify equipment movement, while a rigid foundation may behave very differently.

Engineers should consider whether equipment excitation can interact with structural modes or other mechanical systems. For sensitive applications, structural vibration criteria may be more restrictive than ordinary comfort requirements.

This is particularly relevant to laboratories, healthcare facilities, precision manufacturing, aerospace operations, and other environments where vibration can affect process performance.

BIM and CAD Coordination

BIM 3D CAD modeling can help resolve isolation interfaces before fabrication and installation. Mounting-point locations, support frames, equipment dimensions, clearances, access zones, flexible connectors, and structural interfaces can be coordinated in a digital model.

For custom assemblies, detailed CAD information can also reduce ambiguity between engineering design and fabrication. This becomes increasingly important when equipment has irregular geometry or when multiple disciplines share limited mechanical-room space.

The engineering objective is a continuous and predictable load path from equipment through the isolation and support system into the structure, while preserving the intended vibration-control function.

Vibration Isolation and Seismic Restraint: What Is the Difference?

Vibration isolation and seismic restraint are related in many building projects, but they address different engineering problems. Treating them as interchangeable can lead to inadequate performance in either area.

Vibration Isolation

Vibration isolation is primarily concerned with reducing dynamic vibration transmission between equipment and the supporting structure. Its key variables can include stiffness, damping, natural frequency, static deflection, excitation frequency, and transmissibility.

The isolation system is intentionally compliant within the relevant operating range.

Seismic Restraint

Seismic restraints and anchorage address earthquake-induced movement and force transfer. Depending on the application and applicable code provisions, equipment may require positive attachment to the structure, restraint against lateral movement, or engineered anchorage.

This can create an inherent design challenge: the vibration system needs controlled flexibility, while seismic protection may require controlled restraint.

Coordinating Both Systems

Solutions can include restrained spring isolators, seismic snubbers or restraint assemblies where appropriate, anchor bolts, brackets, structural frames, and other engineered components.

The exact arrangement depends on the equipment, seismic design criteria, support conditions, movement requirements, and applicable code provisions.

For projects governed by the IBC, CBC, or ASCE 7, seismic requirements should be evaluated as part of the equipment-support design. Healthcare projects may introduce additional HCAI requirements and coordination requirements.

The important principle is that vibration isolation and seismic restraint solve different engineering problems and should be designed together when both are required. Adding restraints after an isolation system has been selected can unintentionally restrict movement and change its vibration performance.

Designing and Specifying a Complete Vibration Control System

A complete vibration-control strategy starts with application data and ends with coordinated engineering, fabrication, installation, and verification. The process should identify the vibration source, understand the transmission path, establish performance requirements, select the mounting technology, and coordinate the final assembly with the building and equipment.

Information Required for Engineering Selection

A useful equipment package should include the equipment type, operating weight, dimensions, center of gravity, mounting-point locations, operating speed, RPM range, dynamic loads, and support conditions.

Engineers should also identify whether the equipment is fixed-speed or variable-speed and whether startup, shutdown, or transient conditions create special requirements.

Environmental information is equally important. Outdoor equipment may require weather-resistant materials and protective coatings. Marine or corrosive environments may favor stainless steel or corrosion-resistant components. Temperature and chemical exposure can influence elastomer selection.

For building projects, the package should also identify floor or foundation conditions, connected piping and ductwork, available clearances, maintenance requirements, and seismic design criteria.

Evaluating Engineering Submittals

Procurement teams and specification writers should review more than dimensional compatibility. Important technical information can include rated load per isolator, spring rate or elastomer stiffness, static deflection, natural frequency, damping characteristics, material specifications, corrosion protection, mounting geometry, and restraint provisions.

Installation requirements should also be reviewed. An isolator can perform differently if it is improperly loaded, inadequately leveled, rigidly bridged, or installed with unintended contact between components.

When Custom Vibration Mounting Is Necessary

Standard products may not address every application. Custom vibration isolation mounts can be appropriate where equipment has unusual mounting points, high or uneven loads, restricted clearances, unusual frequencies, specialized environmental requirements, or combined vibration and seismic constraints.

Custom fabrication may involve structural steel equipment bases, stainless steel assemblies, machined components, brackets, plates, strut channels, or specialized support frames.

Frequently Asked Questions About Vibration Mounting and Controls

What is vibration mounting and controls?

Vibration mounting is the use of engineered mechanical interfaces between equipment and its supporting structure to manage dynamic forces and reduce unwanted vibration transmission. Vibration controls encompass the broader strategy, including isolation mounts, springs, elastomers, wire rope systems, acoustic hangers, inertia bases, flexible connections, structural coordination, and other measures.

The correct solution depends on the source of vibration, excitation frequency, equipment weight, support structure, and receiver requirements. Simply selecting a mount based on equipment weight does not establish whether the system will provide the required isolation.

What is the difference between vibration mounts and vibration isolators?

The terms are frequently used interchangeably, particularly in equipment and HVAC specifications. In engineering applications, however, the distinction can depend on the manufacturer's terminology and the function of the component.

A vibration mount generally refers to the physical mounting component supporting equipment, while a vibration isolator emphasizes the component's role in reducing dynamic transmission.

Both may involve rubber, elastomeric, spring, or wire rope construction. What matters technically is the component's load capacity, stiffness, damping, natural frequency, static deflection, geometry, and intended application.

How do I choose the right vibration isolation mount for equipment?

Begin with the equipment's operating weight and determine the actual load carried by each mounting point. Then establish the operating speed, excitation frequencies, variable-speed range, center of gravity, mounting geometry, and dynamic loads.

Next, evaluate the required isolation performance and structural conditions. Static deflection and natural frequency are especially important for low-frequency isolation. Horizontal stiffness and restraint may also matter where equipment can experience lateral forces or overturning.

Environmental factors should be included as well. Temperature, moisture, chemicals, outdoor exposure, corrosion, and marine conditions can influence the appropriate material and mounting configuration.

Are spring isolators better than rubber vibration mounts?

Neither technology is universally better. Spring and elastomeric systems have different mechanical characteristics and are selected according to the application.

Spring isolators can provide substantial static deflection and low natural frequencies, making them useful for many large HVAC and mechanical applications. Rubber and elastomeric mounts can offer compact geometry, damping, and convenient load distribution for suitable equipment.

The selection should consider load capacity, operating frequency, static deflection, damping, horizontal stability, available space, environmental conditions, and equipment requirements.

What equipment commonly requires vibration isolation?

Common applications include pumps, fans, chillers, air handling units, compressors, motors, generators, cooling towers, blowers, and other mechanical equipment.

Industrial applications can include process machinery, manufacturing equipment, CNC machinery, rotating machinery, and specialized production systems. Laboratories and healthcare facilities may require vibration control because sensitive instruments, imaging systems, research equipment, or occupied spaces can be affected by structure-borne vibration.

Marine and aerospace equipment can introduce additional shock, vibration, weight, and environmental requirements that influence isolator selection.

How does static deflection affect vibration isolation?

Static deflection is an important indicator of isolation-system stiffness. In a simplified spring-mass system, greater static deflection generally corresponds to lower natural frequency.

That relationship is important because effective isolation often requires the isolation-system natural frequency to be sufficiently separated from the equipment's excitation frequency.

However, static deflection cannot be evaluated alone. Excessive flexibility can cause movement or stability problems. The engineering design must balance low-frequency isolation with equipment stability, clearances, support conditions, and restraint requirements.

Can vibration isolation be used with seismic restraints?

Yes. Many building projects require both vibration isolation and seismic restraint, particularly where mechanical equipment must remain functional and attached to the structure during an earthquake.

The systems must be coordinated carefully. Vibration isolators are designed to introduce controlled flexibility, while seismic restraints are intended to limit earthquake-induced movement and transfer applicable forces into the supporting structure.

Adding restraints without considering the isolation system can restrict movement and change its dynamic behavior. The seismic and vibration-control designs should therefore be evaluated as an integrated equipment-support assembly.

Does ASCE 7 apply to vibration isolation systems?

ASCE 7 is primarily a structural and seismic design standard rather than a general vibration-isolation standard. Its relevance to equipment mounting can arise from seismic design forces, nonstructural components, equipment anchorage, restraints, and related structural requirements.

The IBC and CBC can establish additional requirements depending on the project location, occupancy, construction type, and adopted code edition. Healthcare projects in California may also involve HCAI requirements.

Consequently, engineers should distinguish between requirements governing vibration performance and requirements governing seismic support and anchorage.

Can existing equipment be retrofitted with vibration mounts?

Existing equipment can sometimes be retrofitted with vibration isolation, but the feasibility must be evaluated from the actual installation conditions.

The assessment should include equipment weight, center of gravity, existing mounting points, operating speed, dynamic behavior, floor or foundation conditions, available clearance, connected piping and ductwork, and seismic requirements.

A retrofit may require modifications to equipment bases, support frames, flexible connections, or anchorage. Existing structural conditions should also be evaluated before changing the equipment-support configuration.

When are custom vibration isolation mounts necessary?

Custom mounting can become appropriate when standard isolators cannot satisfy the equipment's load, geometry, frequency, environmental, or installation requirements.

Examples include unusually high or uneven equipment loads, irregular mounting points, limited vertical clearance, variable operating conditions, specialized marine or industrial environments, high dynamic loads, or combined vibration and seismic requirements.

Custom systems can incorporate engineered isolators with fabricated steel or stainless steel bases, brackets, plates, frames, or other support components. The objective is to create a complete assembly that matches the actual equipment and structural interface rather than forcing the application into an unsuitable standard configuration.

What information is needed to design a vibration isolation system?

At minimum, engineers generally need equipment type, operating weight, dimensions, center of gravity, mounting locations, operating speed, RPM range, and relevant dynamic loads.

Additional information can include structural support conditions, floor or foundation characteristics, required isolation performance, available clearance, connected piping and ductwork, environmental exposure, temperature, corrosion conditions, seismic criteria, and installation constraints.

For variable-speed equipment, the complete operating range is particularly important. For specialized applications, information about shock, transient loading, marine exposure, sensitive equipment criteria, or process requirements may also be necessary.

Can vibration mounting systems also control structure-borne noise?

Vibration isolation can reduce a major pathway by limiting the transfer of mechanical vibration into the building structure. This can reduce structure-borne noise generated by vibrating equipment.

However, vibration isolation is not identical to airborne acoustic treatment. Airborne sound can travel through air and may require acoustic enclosures, barriers, absorptive materials, or other treatments.

A complete noise-control strategy should therefore identify whether the dominant problem is airborne noise, structure-borne vibration, or both. Acoustic hangers, floor isolation, equipment mounts, flexible connections, and structural measures can be selected according to the identified transmission path.

Conclusion

Effective vibration mounting and controls begin with understanding the mechanical behavior of the equipment, not simply selecting a component from a product table. The most reliable approach considers the vibration source, excitation frequency, transmission path, supported mass, stiffness, damping, static deflection, structural response, connected MEP systems, and operating environment as parts of one engineering system.

Spring vibration isolators, rubber and elastomeric mounts, wire rope isolators, acoustic hangers, floor isolation systems, and custom mounting assemblies each have useful application ranges. The correct selection depends on the actual equipment and project requirements. Load capacity is important, but it is only one part of the engineering decision.

Structural coordination is equally important. Equipment bases, floors, foundations, support frames, piping, ductwork, electrical connections, and flexible interfaces must work with the isolation system rather than bypass it. For seismic applications, the vibration-control strategy must also be coordinated with applicable anchorage and restraint requirements under the project's governing codes and standards. ASCE 7, IBC, CBC, and applicable HCAI requirements can influence the structural and seismic portions of equipment support without replacing the need for dedicated vibration engineering.

For complex commercial, industrial, healthcare, marine, and specialized applications, the strongest solution may combine vibration isolation engineering, structural analysis, BIM 3D CAD coordination, seismic calculations, and custom fabrication. Ultimately, successful vibration control is measured by how well the complete system performs under actual operating conditions. When the source, path, mounting interface, structure, connected services, and applicable seismic requirements are evaluated together, vibration isolation becomes a coordinated engineering solution rather than an isolated hardware decision.
