Vibration Isolation: Systems, Isolators, Applications, and Design Principles

anitaa September 30, 2026

Vibration isolation is a fundamental engineering strategy for controlling the transmission of mechanical vibration from equipment into the structures that support it. In commercial buildings, hospitals, industrial facilities, manufacturing plants, marine environments, and other technically demanding applications, mechanical equipment can generate vibration through rotating imbalance, reciprocating motion, fluid forces, motor operation, or changes in operating speed. Without an appropriately designed isolation path, that energy can travel through floors, structural steel, equipment bases, piping, ductwork, and other connected components, potentially contributing to structure-borne noise, occupant disturbance, equipment interaction, and undesirable structural response.

A vibration isolation system introduces an engineered interface between the vibration source and its support structure. Depending on the application, that interface may use steel springs, elastomeric or rubber/metal mounts, wire rope isolators, acoustic hangers, isolation pads, restrained isolators, or custom assemblies. The correct solution depends on much more than equipment weight. Engineers must consider operating frequency, natural frequency, static deflection, dynamic stiffness, damping, load distribution, mounting geometry, support conditions, and the relationship between excitation and system response. The objective is to establish an isolation system that performs appropriately under the actual operating conditions rather than simply selecting a component based on a catalog load rating.

Vibration isolation also needs to be distinguished from seismic restraint. Isolation addresses vibration generated during normal equipment operation, while seismic systems address earthquake-induced movement, anchorage, restraint, and associated structural requirements. A mechanical installation may therefore require both vibration isolation and seismic provisions, with the two designed as an integrated system.

For engineers, contractors, architects, facility managers, and specification writers, effective vibration control begins with understanding the dynamics of the equipment and its supporting structure. The following guide examines the principles behind vibration isolation, major isolator technologies, selection criteria, HVAC and industrial applications, seismic coordination, custom engineering, installation, and specification requirements. The goal is to provide a practical framework for evaluating vibration isolation systems in U.S. construction and industrial projects.

What Is Vibration Isolation?

Vibration isolation is the controlled reduction of mechanical vibration transmitted between a source of excitation and the structure or equipment connected to it. A typical system can be viewed as a chain consisting of the vibration source, the equipment mass, the isolator, the supporting structure, and the surrounding transmission paths. The isolator changes the mechanical relationship between the equipment and its support so that less vibrational energy is transferred into the structure during operation.

Mechanical vibration can originate from many sources. Rotating machinery may generate forces associated with imbalance, while reciprocating equipment can produce periodic forces through changes in motion. Fans, pumps, compressors, chillers, generators, and other mechanical systems may all introduce operational excitation into their supports. Once vibration enters a rigid structural path, it can travel considerable distances through floors, framing, piping, ductwork, and equipment connections.

Isolation should not be confused with damping. Vibration isolation primarily modifies the transmission path between a source and its support, whereas damping dissipates vibrational energy. A practical system may use both mechanisms, but they serve different engineering functions.

Isolation is also different from seismic restraint. An isolator selected to reduce operational vibration is not automatically a seismic anchorage system. During an earthquake, equipment can experience movement and forces that are fundamentally different from those produced during normal operation. Depending on the project, isolation assemblies may therefore need additional restraints, clearances, anchorage, or specially engineered configurations.

This distinction becomes particularly important for MEP equipment in seismic regions. A system may need to achieve an appropriate dynamic response during normal operation while also remaining adequately restrained under project-specific seismic design requirements. Treating vibration isolation and seismic restraint as interchangeable can result in an incomplete design. Effective engineering considers both objectives from the beginning of equipment support design.

How Vibration Isolation Systems Work

The basic behavior of a vibration isolation system can be understood through a mass-spring model. The equipment represents the mass, while the isolator provides a controlled combination of stiffness and damping between that mass and the supporting structure. When the equipment operates, it generates an excitation frequency. The resulting isolation performance depends heavily on the relationship between that excitation frequency and the natural frequency of the equipment-isolator system.

Natural frequency is particularly important because an isolation system does not automatically perform better simply because it is softer. If the operating frequency approaches the system's natural frequency, resonance can occur and vibration response can increase rather than decrease. Once the excitation frequency is sufficiently separated from the natural frequency, the isolator can reduce the amount of vibration transmitted into the supporting structure.

Transmissibility is one of the key concepts used to evaluate this behavior. It describes the relationship between the dynamic excitation and the resulting transmitted response. Engineers use this relationship to determine whether a selected isolator can provide meaningful isolation under the actual operating conditions.

Static deflection is closely connected to spring stiffness and natural frequency. For spring-supported equipment, the amount the isolator deflects under the equipment's static load provides useful information about the system's dynamic characteristics. However, static deflection alone is not enough to characterize every application. Dynamic stiffness, damping, operating frequency, and load conditions can significantly influence real-world performance.

This is why isolator selection should begin with the equipment's operating characteristics rather than with an isolated component specification. A pump operating at a particular speed presents a different dynamic problem from a slow reciprocating machine or a piece of equipment exposed to shock loading. Variable-speed machinery adds another consideration because its excitation frequency can change during operation.

The complete engineering relationship can therefore be viewed as:

Vibration source → dynamic load → isolator stiffness → static deflection → natural frequency → damping → transmissibility → structural response

This sequence provides a useful framework for evaluating whether an isolation system is appropriate for a specific piece of equipment.

Key Engineering Parameters for Vibration Isolation Design

Selecting a vibration isolation system requires a complete understanding of the equipment and its support conditions. Equipment weight is an obvious starting point, but the operating weight must be considered together with the center of gravity, mounting locations, and load distribution. If four isolators support a machine with an uneven center of gravity, each isolator may carry a different load. A nominal system capacity therefore does not by itself establish whether the arrangement will perform correctly.

Static deflection is another important parameter, particularly for spring isolators. It reflects the amount of displacement produced by the equipment's static load and is related to isolator stiffness and natural frequency. Engineers must establish the required performance rather than selecting deflection solely because a particular value appears commonly in product catalogs.

Dynamic loads also matter. Rotating imbalance, reciprocating forces, startup and shutdown conditions, variable-speed operation, fluid-induced forces, and process loads can change the dynamic behavior of equipment. A system that performs adequately at one operating point may require additional evaluation if the equipment operates across a broad speed range.

Damping and dynamic stiffness add another layer of analysis. Two isolation systems with similar static characteristics can behave differently under dynamic excitation because their material properties and frequency-dependent response differ. Elastomeric materials, springs, wire rope assemblies, and other isolation technologies each have characteristic mechanical behavior that must be considered in context.

The supporting structure is equally important. A vibration isolation system mounted on a concrete slab, structural steel frame, rooftop platform, suspended support, or inertia base interacts with that support differently. Structural flexibility, local stiffness, equipment anchorage, and connection details can influence the final response.

Environmental conditions should also enter the design process. Marine installations, industrial facilities, and exposed mechanical equipment may require materials and finishes appropriate for corrosion, moisture, temperature, or other environmental conditions. The design objective is not simply to identify an isolator that can carry the equipment; it is to develop an isolation assembly that remains suitable for the complete operating environment.

Types of Vibration Isolators

Different isolation technologies address different combinations of load, frequency, displacement, environment, and installation requirements. There is no single isolator type that is appropriate for every mechanical system, so selection should be based on the actual dynamic and structural conditions.

Spring Vibration Isolators

Spring isolators are commonly considered for heavy mechanical equipment because they can accommodate significant loads while providing relatively low stiffness and substantial static deflection. Applications can include chillers, air-handling units, pumps, fans, compressors, generators, and other large mechanical equipment.

Rubber and Elastomeric Isolators

Rubber and elastomeric mounts can provide compact isolation solutions where space, equipment configuration, and required dynamic characteristics favor an elastomeric approach. Their properties depend on the compound, geometry, loading, temperature, and operating conditions. Rubber/metal assemblies can combine elastomeric elements with fabricated metal components to create a practical mounting interface.

Wire Rope Isolators

Wire rope isolators use formed metal cable assemblies to provide multidirectional vibration and shock isolation. Their characteristics can make them useful for aerospace, marine, industrial, and other environments where equipment may experience complex loading or harsh conditions. Material selection can also be important when corrosion resistance is required.

Acoustic Hangers

Acoustic or vibration isolation hangers are used with suspended HVAC equipment, piping, ductwork, and other overhead systems. They can help interrupt the direct mechanical transmission path between suspended equipment and the building structure.

Floor Isolation and Pads

Floor-mounted isolation systems can incorporate isolation pads, bases, rails, or other assemblies beneath equipment. These approaches are useful where equipment is supported directly from slabs or fabricated bases.

Captive and Restrained Isolators

Captive or restrained configurations can provide controlled movement while maintaining an isolation function. They can be particularly relevant where operational vibration control must coexist with movement limitations or project-specific restraint requirements.

The important engineering question is not which technology is universally superior, but which technology provides the required combination of stiffness, deflection, damping, load capacity, movement control, environmental durability, and installation compatibility for the equipment in question.

How to Select a Vibration Isolation System

A practical selection process should begin with the vibration source and proceed toward the equipment, support structure, and project requirements. The first step is identifying the type of excitation. Rotating machinery, reciprocating machinery, fans, pumps, compressors, HVAC equipment, manufacturing equipment, and marine machinery can produce different vibration characteristics.

The next step is establishing operating conditions. Engineers should review operating speed, frequency range, startup and shutdown behavior, variable-speed operation, shock loading, and duty cycle. Variable-frequency drives deserve particular attention because equipment may operate across multiple speeds rather than at a single steady-state frequency.

Equipment loads should then be established. The analysis should include operating weight, individual mounting-point loads, center of gravity, dynamic loading, and load distribution. For unusual equipment, obtaining accurate manufacturer data is particularly important.

The support structure must also be evaluated. A concrete slab, structural steel frame, rooftop support, equipment platform, suspended support, or inertia base can each create different design conditions. The isolation system should work with the support rather than being evaluated as a completely independent component.

After these parameters are established, the engineer can compare spring, elastomeric, rubber/metal, wire rope, hanger, and floor-mounted solutions. The comparison should consider natural frequency, static deflection, dynamic stiffness, damping, available clearance, environmental conditions, and expected movement.

Finally, seismic requirements should be coordinated with the isolation design. In applicable projects, equipment may require seismic restraint, anchorage, clearance, or restrained isolation assemblies in accordance with the project's adopted codes and design criteria.

This approach reduces the risk of selecting an isolator based only on equipment weight or a generic application category. The goal is to match the isolation system to the complete dynamic and structural problem.

Vibration Isolation for HVAC and MEP Systems

HVAC and MEP equipment represents one of the most common applications for vibration isolation because mechanical equipment can transmit vibration directly into building structures and connected services. Chillers, air-handling units, fans, pumps, cooling towers, rooftop equipment, compressors, suspended equipment, piping, and ductwork may all require consideration of vibration transmission.

For a typical HVAC installation, isolator selection cannot be separated from the surrounding MEP connections. A properly isolated piece of equipment can still transmit vibration through rigid piping, duct connections, conduits, structural supports, or other paths that effectively bypass the intended isolation system. This condition is sometimes described as a short circuit in the isolation path.

Flexible connections may therefore be necessary where appropriate to preserve the intended mechanical separation. Their selection and installation must be coordinated with equipment movement, pressure, temperature, structural support, and project requirements.

Equipment bases are another important consideration. Heavy chillers, pumps, and other machinery may use inertia bases or fabricated support assemblies to distribute loads and provide an appropriate interface with the isolators. The base, isolators, equipment, and support structure should be evaluated as a complete system.

Rooftop installations can introduce additional challenges because equipment may interact with structural framing, roof curbs, architectural assemblies, and weather-exposed conditions. Suspended HVAC equipment presents a different configuration in which acoustic hangers or other isolation hangers may be integrated into the support system.

Healthcare facilities require particularly careful coordination because vibration can affect occupied areas and sensitive equipment. Mechanical rooms may be located near patient-care spaces, laboratories, imaging environments, or other areas where vibration and structure-borne noise are important considerations.

The same engineering principles apply across these applications: identify the excitation, establish equipment loads, evaluate operating frequencies, select an appropriate isolation technology, and coordinate the isolation path with structural and MEP connections.

Industrial, Healthcare, and Specialized Applications

Industrial vibration isolation often involves more demanding combinations of continuous operation, high dynamic loads, shock, and environmental exposure. Manufacturing presses, pumps, compressors, production machinery, and rotating equipment can create substantial dynamic forces that must be considered when designing the equipment support.

For industrial machinery, the operating cycle is important. Equipment that starts, stops, changes speed, or experiences intermittent process loading can behave differently from continuously rotating equipment. The isolation system may need to accommodate changes in excitation as well as steady-state operation.

Aerospace and marine applications can introduce additional requirements. Equipment may experience multidirectional excitation, shock, vibration, limited installation space, and corrosive environments. Wire rope isolators and specialized elastomeric or fabricated assemblies may be considered where their characteristics align with those conditions. Material selection can become particularly important for stainless steel or other corrosion-resistant components.

Sensitive equipment presents another category. Laboratories, instrumentation, precision machinery, and specialized medical environments may have low tolerance for environmental vibration. In these situations, engineers must consider not only the vibration generated by the equipment itself but also vibration arriving from surrounding building systems.

Healthcare facilities can require coordinated design across mechanical, structural, architectural, and equipment disciplines. Mechanical equipment supporting a hospital may need vibration control while also satisfying project-specific seismic requirements and facility standards. The same principle applies to data centers, laboratories, cleanrooms, and other critical environments where equipment performance and facility continuity are important.

These applications demonstrate why vibration isolation is fundamentally an engineering problem rather than a simple hardware-selection exercise. The appropriate solution depends on the source, operating conditions, equipment, structure, environment, and required performance.

For projects involving unusual geometry, complex loads, or specialized environmental requirements, custom fabrication can provide the structural interface needed to integrate isolation components with the equipment and building.

Vibration Isolation and Seismic Requirements

Operational vibration control and seismic protection should be treated as related but distinct design objectives. Vibration isolation addresses forces generated while equipment operates. Seismic design addresses earthquake-induced movement, forces, restraint, anchorage, and the behavior of nonstructural components during a seismic event.

This distinction is especially important in California and other seismic regions. A spring isolator or elastomeric mount may be selected to achieve a desired dynamic response during normal equipment operation, but that component alone does not establish that the equipment satisfies applicable seismic requirements.

Depending on the project, equipment may require restrained isolators, seismic restraints, anchorage hardware, structural support modifications, or other provisions. The final configuration depends on the equipment, building, location, occupancy, structural system, and applicable design criteria.

ASCE 7 provides important provisions for seismic design, while the International Building Code and California Building Code establish requirements that may apply depending on the adopted jurisdiction and project. Healthcare facilities subject to California requirements may also involve HCAI considerations. OSHPD terminology remains relevant to many professionals because the former Office of Statewide Health Planning and Development functions associated with healthcare facility construction are now administered through HCAI.

The engineering process should therefore begin by identifying applicable requirements rather than assuming that a generic isolation detail is sufficient. Seismic calculations, anchorage design, support conditions, clearances, and restraint configurations may need to be coordinated with the vibration isolation system.

The central objective is integration. An isolation system should preserve its intended operational function while the overall equipment-support assembly addresses the applicable seismic design requirements. Where isolation and restraint interact, the configuration should be engineered rather than improvised during installation.

Custom Vibration Isolation Systems

Standard vibration isolators can be effective when equipment geometry, loading, operating characteristics, and environmental conditions fall within established design ranges. However, projects frequently involve conditions that do not fit a simple catalog configuration. Unusual equipment geometry, high dynamic loads, irregular load distribution, nonstandard mounting footprints, limited clearance, harsh environments, marine exposure, and specialized operating frequencies can all justify a custom engineering approach.

A custom vibration isolation system may combine isolation elements with structural frames, inertia bases, mounting brackets, rails, custom strut channels, or other fabricated components. Stainless steel, carbon steel, aluminum, and structural steel can be selected according to the project's mechanical, structural, environmental, and fabrication requirements.

Custom engineering is particularly useful when the equipment has an unusual center of gravity or when standard mounting points do not align with the supporting structure. A fabricated base can distribute equipment loads across multiple isolators while providing defined mounting interfaces.

BIM and 3D CAD modeling can further support coordination. A digital model can establish equipment footprints, isolator locations, structural interfaces, clearances, MEP connections, and fabricated components before field installation. This can help identify spatial conflicts that may otherwise emerge during construction.

Installation, Specification, and Performance Considerations

A correctly selected isolator can underperform if the installed assembly does not preserve the intended isolation path. Installation quality is therefore part of vibration isolation engineering rather than a separate concern.

Load distribution is one of the first considerations. Each isolator should receive the load for which it was selected, and equipment should be properly positioned and leveled. Changes to equipment weight, accessories, piping, or other connected components can alter the actual loading after the original design.

Isolation can also be bypassed by rigid connections. Piping, ductwork, conduit, structural attachments, or other components that create unintended rigid paths can transmit vibration around the isolator. Flexible connections should therefore be coordinated where necessary to maintain the intended isolation strategy.

Clearances are also important. Equipment movement under operating or transient conditions must be considered where applicable. Restrained or captive systems require appropriate restraint configuration and hardware installation. Seismic restraints must not be treated as an afterthought that unintentionally compromises the operational isolation system.

For specifications and submittals, engineers should identify the equipment manufacturer and model, operating weight, mounting footprint, center of gravity, operating speed, frequency range, dynamic loads, required isolation performance, support structure, environmental conditions, seismic design requirements, corrosion requirements, installation constraints, and required calculations.

The specification should communicate performance requirements clearly enough that the proposed system can be evaluated on engineering criteria rather than simply on product name. This is particularly important when several isolation technologies could potentially satisfy the application.

Frequently Asked Questions About Vibration Isolation

What is vibration isolation?

Vibration isolation is an engineering method used to reduce the transmission of mechanical vibration from equipment into its supporting structure or connected systems. An isolator creates a controlled mechanical interface between the vibration source and the support. The system's effectiveness depends on parameters such as equipment mass, isolator stiffness, natural frequency, damping, excitation frequency, and transmissibility.

The objective is not necessarily to eliminate all vibration. Instead, engineers evaluate how much vibration is generated, how it is transmitted, and what level of response is acceptable for the application. The isolation system is then selected and configured to provide the required dynamic behavior.

How does a vibration isolation system work?

A vibration isolation system typically uses a spring, elastomeric element, wire rope assembly, or another compliant component between equipment and its support. This changes the stiffness and dynamic relationship between the equipment mass and structure.

The most important relationships involve natural frequency and excitation frequency. If operating excitation is sufficiently separated from the isolation system's natural frequency, transmission can be reduced. If excitation approaches the natural frequency, resonance can increase the response.

For this reason, engineers need more information than equipment weight alone. Operating speed, dynamic loading, mounting configuration, static deflection, damping, and support conditions all contribute to the final system response.

What is the difference between vibration isolation and vibration damping?

Vibration isolation and damping address vibration through different mechanisms. Isolation modifies the mechanical transmission path between a source and its support. Damping dissipates vibrational energy within the system.

An isolation system can incorporate damping, but the two terms should not be treated as interchangeable. A highly damped system may behave differently from a low-damping isolation system, particularly around resonance. Engineers therefore consider both properties when evaluating dynamic performance.

What type of vibration isolator is best for HVAC equipment?

There is no universal isolator that is appropriate for every HVAC application. Selection depends on equipment weight, operating frequency, static deflection requirements, mounting arrangement, dynamic loading, structural support, available clearance, and project-specific requirements.

Spring isolators may be considered for heavy chillers, air-handling units, pumps, fans, and other mechanical equipment. Elastomeric mounts may be suitable for applications where their stiffness and geometry match the equipment requirements. Acoustic hangers may be appropriate for suspended HVAC systems.

The correct selection should come from the equipment's actual operating conditions rather than from the equipment category alone.

Are spring isolators better than rubber vibration isolators?

Spring and rubber isolators have different mechanical characteristics, so the comparison should be made against the project requirements rather than treated as a universal ranking.

Springs can provide relatively low stiffness and substantial deflection for heavy equipment, while elastomeric isolators can provide compact mounting solutions with characteristics determined by their material and geometry. Load range, natural frequency, damping, operating environment, available space, equipment movement, and installation configuration all influence the appropriate choice.

When should wire rope isolators be used?

Wire rope isolators can be considered where vibration and shock occur across multiple axes or where equipment operates in demanding environments. They are used in applications that can include aerospace, marine, industrial, and specialized machinery.

Their construction can provide useful characteristics for multidirectional motion and shock loading, while material selection can address environmental requirements. The specific wire rope configuration still needs to be matched to equipment mass, loading, excitation, displacement, and mounting conditions.

Do vibration isolators provide seismic protection?

A vibration isolator should not automatically be assumed to provide seismic protection. Vibration isolation addresses operational excitation, while seismic protection addresses earthquake-induced forces, movement, anchorage, and restraint.

Depending on the project, an isolation system may need seismic restraints, captive configurations, anchorage, clearances, or other engineered provisions. Applicable requirements can depend on the adopted building code, ASCE 7 provisions, project location, occupancy, equipment classification, and structural design.

What is static deflection in a vibration isolation system?

Static deflection is the displacement produced when the equipment's static load is applied to the isolator. For spring-based systems, static deflection is related to spring stiffness and contributes to the resulting natural frequency of the equipment-isolator system.

It is an important selection parameter, but it should not be considered in isolation. Engineers may also need to evaluate dynamic stiffness, damping, operating frequency, load distribution, and equipment movement.

How is vibration transmissibility evaluated?

Transmissibility describes how vibration response is transferred through an isolation system relative to the excitation. Engineers evaluate the relationship between excitation frequency and the system's natural frequency, along with the effect of damping.

The basic principle is that isolation performance depends strongly on frequency separation. Near resonance, response can increase. At sufficiently higher excitation-to-natural-frequency ratios, transmission can be reduced. Actual performance depends on the isolation system and operating conditions, so a simple load-capacity comparison is not enough.

When does equipment require custom vibration isolation?

Custom vibration isolation may be appropriate when equipment does not fit standard mounting configurations or when operating conditions require a specialized assembly. Examples include unusual equipment geometry, irregular load distribution, high dynamic loads, limited clearance, marine or corrosive environments, specialized operating frequencies, and unusual mounting footprints.

A custom system can combine standard isolation elements with fabricated structural components such as inertia bases, frames, rails, brackets, and mounting assemblies. Engineering data should be established before developing the final configuration.

Can vibration isolation systems be used with seismic restraints?

Yes, vibration isolation and seismic restraint can be integrated when the system is engineered for both requirements. Restrained spring isolators, captive configurations, separate seismic restraints, anchorage systems, and other approaches may be considered depending on the equipment and project requirements.

The critical issue is maintaining the intended operational isolation while providing the required seismic restraint. A restraint that unintentionally creates a rigid vibration path can reduce isolation performance, so the complete assembly should be coordinated during design.

What information is needed to select a vibration isolator?

Important information includes equipment type and manufacturer, operating weight, mounting footprint, center of gravity, mounting-point loads, operating speed, frequency range, dynamic loads, support structure, required isolation performance, environmental conditions, corrosion exposure, available clearance, and seismic requirements.

For custom systems, additional information may include equipment drawings, structural interfaces, mounting details, BIM models, installation constraints, and fabrication requirements. The more accurately these conditions are established, the more precisely the isolation system can be matched to the application.

Conclusion

Effective vibration isolation begins with understanding that an isolator is not simply a component placed beneath a machine. It is part of a dynamic system involving equipment mass, excitation, stiffness, damping, natural frequency, support conditions, and transmission paths. The engineering objective is to control how mechanical energy moves from the equipment into the surrounding structure while maintaining appropriate equipment stability and operational performance.

For HVAC and MEP systems, this means evaluating chillers, pumps, fans, air-handling units, compressors, rooftop equipment, piping, ductwork, and suspended systems as interconnected assemblies. For industrial and marine equipment, the analysis may also need to account for shock, multidirectional loads, variable operating conditions, corrosion, and unusual mounting geometry. Healthcare and other critical facilities can require additional coordination because vibration and structure-borne noise may affect sensitive spaces and equipment.

Spring isolators, elastomeric mounts, rubber/metal assemblies, wire rope isolators, acoustic hangers, floor isolation systems, and restrained configurations each have different engineering characteristics. Selecting among them requires actual project data rather than a generic assumption that one technology is suitable for every application.

Vibration isolation must also be coordinated with seismic design where applicable. IBC, CBC, ASCE 7, and project-specific California or HCAI requirements can influence equipment restraint, anchorage, support design, and the relationship between isolation and seismic protection. Operational vibration control and seismic restraint should remain distinct engineering objectives while being coordinated within the same equipment-support design.

When standard products do not match equipment geometry, loading, environmental conditions, or project constraints, custom engineering and fabrication can extend the available design options. Ultimately, the strongest vibration isolation strategy is the one developed from the actual equipment, operating conditions, structure, environment, and project requirements. A disciplined engineering process allows contractors, designers, facility teams, and procurement professionals to evaluate isolation systems based on measurable technical criteria and coordinate the final assembly with the broader structural and MEP design.
